Bakra Eid

Eid al-Adha, commonly called Bakra Eid in South Asia, is one of the two major Islamic festivals celebrated by Muslims around the world. The name “Bakra Eid” comes from the Urdu/Hindi word bakra (goat), because animals are traditionally sacrificed during the festival. The holiday commemorates the devotion and obedience of the Prophet Ibrahim (Abraham), who was willing to sacrifice his son as an act of submission to Allah. According to Islamic belief, Allah replaced the son with a ram before the sacrifice took place. Main Traditions of Bakra Eid Muslims begin the day with a special congregational prayer at mosques or open grounds. Families sacrifice animals such as goats, sheep, cows, or camels, depending on local customs and affordability. The meat is usually divided into three parts: For the family For relatives and friends For people in need People wear new or clean clothes, visit relatives, and share meals together. Religious Importance

Bakra Eid teaches: Sacrifice and devotion to God Charity and helping the poor Gratitude and community spirit When It Is Celebrated Eid al-Adha takes place on the 10th day of the Islamic month of Dhu al-Hijjah, during the annual pilgrimage of Hajj in Mecca. Because the Islamic calendar is lunar, the date changes every year in the Gregorian calendar. In countries like Pakistan, the festival is celebrated with great enthusiasm, including animal markets, family gatherings, and traditional dishes made from fresh meat.
